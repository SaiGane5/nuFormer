nuFormer project · synthgen/

How the synthetic bank customers are made

No real bank data was available, so the project builds its own customers from 12 simple rules. Because we wrote the rules, we also know each customer's true chance of taking every product. Real data can never tell you that.

Stage 1

Who they are

01

Correlated personality traits

Each customer gets seven hidden scores: income, saver, travel, financial stress, digital, discretionary spending and age. They are drawn together, so they hang together the way people do: savers earn more and are rarely stressed.

Why: independent traits would create impossible people, like a stressed high-saver, and make the signal unrealistic. Visible facts (kids, car, rent, payday, subscriptions) are then derived from these traits.

02

One life event, often recent

60% of customers get one life event. Which one depends on their traits: stressed people get money trouble, younger people get a baby. The date is skewed toward the recent past.

Share of customers in a 3,000-customer sample. 31.5% had their event in the 30 days before scoring.

p(event) = 0.60day ~ Gamma(1.3, 30)≤ 1 event per customer

Why: recent events are what a 90-day total blurs and a transaction sequence shows clearly. This is the signal the language model is meant to find.

What they spend

Every customer gets 150 days of history before their scoring date: a median of 205 transactions.

04

Fixed monthly calendar

Regular money lands on fixed days every month. Life events change it: a raise lifts salary 15–40%, and 35% of stressed customers lose their job, so salary stops.

1/5/10/15/30Salary (payday by customer) payday + 2Invest 8–30% of income 5–10Rent (55% pay rent) 12 · 18 · 20Electricity · water · phone any day0–5 subscriptions day 1Interest for savers

Why: real statements are dominated by these rhythms, and their changes (salary up, salary gone, first investment) are strong signals.

05

Random daily spending, shaped by traits and events

For each category and each day, the number of purchases is a Poisson draw. Its rate is a base rate × a trait multiplier × an event multiplier. After an event, behaviour changes from that day on.

06

Income sets the price and the shop

Richer customers pay more at the same merchant, and they pick premium merchants more often.

amount = e^( μmerchant + 0.45·log(income ÷ 4,000) + σ·ε ) P(shop) ∝ e^( 0.9 × income score × tier )

Example from the sample: a customer earning R$21,585/month buys groceries at St Marche and Pão de Açúcar and eats at Outback. A customer earning R$1,949 shops at the corner mercadinho.

Why: merchant names carry information that the profile's coarse totals throw away.

07

Trips as bursts

Trips arrive as clusters, not single purchases. A flight is booked 7–40 days ahead, then a hotel within 4 days of that. An international trip adds 3–8 days of card use abroad. Stressed customers travel half as often.

08

Messy statement text

Descriptions are written the way a bank statement prints them: uppercase, with a city, a store number or a person's name filled into a template. 5% are cut short at 8–18 characters, as real statements often are.

Stage 3

What's true

09

A true chance for every product

Each product has a simple scoring formula over the hidden traits and recent events. Random noise is added, and each product's intercept is tuned so that 8% of customers would take it on average. The result is each customer's true probability for all five products.

Travel card = 1.6·travel + 0.6·income − 0.5·stress Personal loan = 1.4·stress + 1.6·[stress event ≤ 30 days] − 0.6·saver Investments = 1.5·saver + 0.7·income + 1.2·[raise ≤ 30 days] − 0.8·stress Insurance = 1.0·family + 1.6·[new car or baby ≤ 45 days] + 0.3·age Cashback plus = 1.2·discretionary share + 0.5·digital + noise N(0, 0.5), then probability = sigmoid(score + intercept)

Why: with the true probabilities known, we can compute the best AUC any model could reach (0.87). That tells us how much signal is left for a model to find.

10

One offer, one coin flip

Like a real campaign, each customer is offered one product. 30% of offers are targeted (the product with the highest noisy score) and 70% are random. Whether the customer accepts is a coin flip weighted by their true chance for that product.

Why: real banks only see the outcome of the offer they made. Mixing targeted and random offers copies the bias of a real campaign, and gives an overall acceptance rate of 12.7%.

12

Split by time, then checked

Scoring dates are spread over 12 months. Models train on the first 8 months, are tuned on month 9 and are tested on months 10–12, so every test is on future customers. A validator then refuses to write data that breaks any rule.
